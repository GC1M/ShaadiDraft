Wedding invitation wording for every ceremony

Guide · Updated November 2026

An Indian wedding is rarely one event. Between the engagement, Haldi, Mehendi, Sangeet, the wedding itself and the reception, a family might send four or five invitations, and each one has a different tone. The wedding card is formal and full of blessings. The Haldi card can be playful. The reception card is short and polite. This guide walks through what each card needs to say, who should be named as the host, and sample lines you can adapt.

What every invitation needs

Whatever the ceremony, guests need the same five facts, and they should be easy to find at a glance:

  • The event. "Haldi", "Sangeet Sandhya", "Shubh Vivah". Put it in the largest text after the couple's names.
  • Who is getting married. The bride's and groom's names, usually with their parents' names underneath.
  • Date and day. Write the weekday as well as the date. Older relatives often remember "the Sunday wedding" more easily than the number.
  • Time. For a wedding, give the arrival time of the baraat or jaan and the muhurat separately, so guests know when to arrive and when the main ritual happens.
  • Venue with a usable address. The hall name alone is not enough for guests from out of town. Add the road and area, and a landmark if the place is hard to find.

Finish with a contact line: a name and phone number guests can call with questions. On Gujarati and Hindi cards this is often part of the list of people inviting the guests.

Who is the host?

Traditionally the invitation comes from the parents or the eldest members of the family, not the couple. A typical wedding line reads "Mrs. Sunita and Mr. Rajesh Patel request the pleasure of your company at the wedding of their daughter". Many urban couples now host their own celebrations and write "Kripa and Harsh invite you to celebrate their wedding". Both are correct. Pick the one that matches who is actually hosting, and stay consistent across all the cards in the set.

When both families host jointly, name both: "The Patel and Desai families invite you". This is especially common for the reception.

The invocation

Most Hindu wedding cards open with a line to Ganesha, the remover of obstacles, such as ॥ श्री गणेशाय नमः ॥, sometimes followed by the shlok beginning Vakratunda Mahakaya. Families devoted to another deity use their own line: Shri Radhe Krishna, an invocation to Venkateshwara (Balaji) on many Tamil and Telugu cards, ੴ ਸਤਿ ਨਾਮੁ on Sikh Anand Karaj cards, or the Bismillah on Muslim Nikkah cards. If your family prefers no religious line, a floral motif in the same position keeps the card balanced. The invocation usually appears only on the wedding card and sometimes the engagement card; Haldi, Mehendi and Sangeet cards often skip it.

Haldi

Haldi is a morning ceremony at home or a small venue, attended mostly by close family. The card can be bright and informal. Mention the dress code if you have one, since yellow outfits are common and guests like to know.

Join us for the Haldi of Kripa
Saturday, 21 November 2026, 10:00 am
Patel residence, Ring Road, Surat
Come dressed in yellow, and expect to get some on you.

In Tamil families the equivalent bathing ritual is Mangala Snanam, and in Gujarati families it is the Pithi. Use the name your family uses; guests from the community will look for it.

Mehendi

Mehendi is usually an afternoon or early evening gathering where the bride and female relatives have henna applied. The card should say how long it runs, because guests often come and go.

Henna, music and chai
Mehendi for Kripa
Saturday, 21 November, 3:00 pm onwards
Shreeji Party Plot, Varachha, Surat

Sangeet

The Sangeet is the party of the wedding week: dance performances, songs, and in Gujarati families often raas garba. It is usually the most relaxed card in the set. If family members are performing, say so; it tells guests the evening has a programme and they should arrive on time.

An evening of music, dance and garba
Sangeet Sandhya
Saturday, 21 November, 8:00 pm
Dinner to follow

The wedding

The wedding card is the formal one and carries the most information. A common structure, top to bottom:

  1. Invocation or shlok.
  2. A line of welcome from the hosts: "With the blessings of our elders, we invite you and your family".
  3. The bride's name, her parents' names, then the groom's name and his parents' names. Prefixes such as Chi. (Chiranjeevi) for the couple and A.Sau. for married women are traditional on Hindi, Marathi and Gujarati cards.
  4. Date, day and muhurat time, with the baraat or jaan arrival listed separately.
  5. Venue and address.
  6. The names of the family members who are inviting you, and a contact number.
With the blessings of our elders
Mrs. Sunita and Mr. Rajesh Patel
invite you and your family to the wedding of their daughter
Kripa
with
Harsh
son of Mrs. Nita and Mr. Vikram Desai
Sunday, 22 November 2026
Baraat arrival 6:00 pm · Pheras 7:30 pm
Shreeji Party Plot, Ring Road, Varachha, Surat

If you are sending one card that covers several functions, add a second page listing each event with its own date, time and venue. Guests save one file and have the whole schedule.

Reception

The reception is often hosted by the groom's family, or by both families together, and is the event to which colleagues and wider acquaintances are invited. Keep it short. The invocation and long family lists usually stay on the wedding card.

The Patel and Desai families request the pleasure of your company
at the reception of Kripa and Harsh
Monday, 23 November 2026, 8:00 pm
Dinner and celebrations

Small things that cause big confusion

  • Midnight muhurats. If the ritual is at 12:30 am, write the date of the evening guests arrive and add "night of 22 November". Otherwise half the family turns up a day late.
  • "With family" or not. Phrases like sahkutumb (with the whole family) or sajode (with your spouse) tell guests exactly who is invited. Use them deliberately.
  • Spellings. Ask both families to check every name. A misspelled grandparent's name is the error people remember.
  • Two languages. If guests are split between, say, Gujarati-speaking elders and English-speaking friends, make the wedding card in the family language and the reception card in English rather than squeezing both onto one page.
